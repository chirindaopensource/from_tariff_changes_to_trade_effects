# From Tariff Changes to Trade Effects: Executive Summary

**Source:** Maria Cipollina (University of Molise; Centro Rossi-Doria, Università Roma Tre) and Luca De Benedictis (Universitas Mercatorum; Centro Rossi-Doria, Università Roma Tre; Luiss University; CRIAVET), *From Tariff Changes to Trade Effects: A Practical Framework for Empirical Trade Policy Analysis*. Prepared for the 15th AIEAA Annual Conference, in memory of Luca Salvatici.

---

## 1. Introduction

For decades, trade policy was framed as progressive liberalization. Multilateral rounds lowered most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariffs, and preferential agreements granted selected partners reduced or duty-free access. The empirical toolkit was built around that experiment and its effects on bilateral trade, trade creation and diversion, and welfare.

The 2020s reverse the narrative. Since 2018 the United States has raised tariffs under Sections 201, 232 and 301, the largest increase in US import protection in decades, with documented effects on prices, welfare, employment, supply chains and trade diversion.

The paper argues that this change alters the empirical question itself, not merely the sign of the shock. A bilateral tariff cut improves one exporter's access *relative to its competitors*. An MFN tariff increase raises costs for a potentially broad set of suppliers while leaving preferential tariffs unchanged, and therefore changes the competitive position of exporters whose own tariff has not moved. In a system of overlapping preferential agreements, market access is inherently relative, and **liberalization and protectionism are not symmetric policy experiments**.

## 2. Research Question and Contribution

The paper does not propose a new protection index or a new structural trade model. It asks a logically prior question: *what is required to translate an observed or counterfactual tariff change into an economically meaningful estimate of its trade effects?*

The answer rests on distinguishing three related but conceptually distinct objects:

1. the **policy instrument** that changes (the applied tariff);
2. the resulting **change in relative market access**; and
3. the **trade response** generated by that change.

The central claim is that the impact of an MFN tariff increase can be inferred neither from the bilateral tariff change alone nor, in general, from a liberalization exercise with the sign reversed.

## 3. The Literature Space

To make heterogeneous studies comparable, the authors map the recent literature as a **two-mode (affiliation) network** in four steps.

| Step | Procedure |
|---|---|
| 1. Selection | Seventeen studies: work on the 2018–19 US tariffs and resulting diversion, two studies of the 2025–26 tariff wave, elasticity-focused work, and methodological antecedents. Cipollina and Salvatici (2008, 2026) and Cipollina and De Benedictis (2026) share one design and form a single entry. The authors acknowledge that the selection is discretionary. |
| 2. Classification | Each abstract, and only the abstract, is coded on nine elements: MFN or schedule-wide tariff shock; relative position of all suppliers; preferential regimes; third-country reallocation; structural or general-equilibrium framework; estimated (not assumed) trade elasticity; prices and welfare; supply-chain linkages; quasi-experimental identification. Each element scores 1 (claimed), 0.5 (partial or implicit) or 0 (absent). Three elements common to almost all abstracts (tariff change as the source of variation, product-level data, the US as importer) are excluded because they delimit the field rather than divide it. |
| 3. Distance | The resulting $17 \times 9$ matrix is compared with the Gower (1971) distance, the mean absolute difference across the nine elements, ranging from 0 (identical claims) to 1 (no shared claims). |
| 4. Visualisation | Elements are fixed on a circle. Each study is placed at the claim-weighted barycentre of the elements it claims, and a small repulsion term separates overlapping nodes without altering their ordering (Breiger, 1974; Borgatti and Everett, 1997). |

**Findings from the mapping.**

- The studies closest to Cipollina and De Benedictis (2026) are Cipollina et al. (2017) together with Cipollina and Salvatici (2020), at a distance of 0.11, and Fugazza and Nicita (2013), at 0.28. Every other study lies at 0.33 or beyond.
- The literature is organised around **two poles**:
  - *Reduced-form diversion studies* measure where displaced imports go, but treat the tariff shock as a product list and leave the schedule unmodelled.
  - *Structural and elasticity-based work* models the schedule, but rarely measures reallocation across suppliers.
- The reference paper is the only one that connects preferential regimes, third-country reallocation and a structural treatment of the tariff schedule at full strength. It claims five of the nine elements without qualification, against a maximum of four for any other study.
- It does not claim quasi-experimental identification, the strength of the reduced-form pole. The two poles are therefore better read as complements than as alternatives. The exercise maps what each study *claims*, not how well it is executed.

## 4. Methods of Analysis

### 4.1 From tariff changes to relative market access

For product $k$, exporter $i$ and time $t$, the most immediate measure of a reform is the change in the applied bilateral tariff, $\Delta\tau_{ik} = \tau_{ik1} - \tau_{ik0}$. This measure is incomplete for two reasons:

- Equal changes in tariff *rates* need not imply equal changes in tariff-inclusive prices. Concessions are therefore better expressed in **tariff factors** $(1+\tau)$ than in percentage-point differences.
- The same bilateral change has different implications depending on how competitors are treated. An exporter with $\Delta\tau_{ik} = 0$ can still experience a change in relative market access.

Measuring competitive advantage therefore requires a benchmark reflecting the tariffs actually faced by competitors, rather than the MFN rate. Following Cipollina et al. (2017), this raises two distinct problems: a **definition bias** (the choice of reference tariff) and an **aggregation bias** (how heterogeneous supplier treatments are combined into that reference).

Under CES preferences, the reference tariff is a power mean of tariff factors:

$$
1 + T_{kt} = \left[ \sum_{i} \alpha_i \, (1 + \tau_{ikt})^{1-\sigma_k} \right]^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma_k}}, \qquad \sigma_k > 1, \qquad \sum_i \alpha_i = 1,
$$

where $\sigma_k$ is the elasticity of substitution across origins and $\alpha_i$ is the normalised CES weight. The **bilateral tariff margin** is

$$
btm_{ikt} = \frac{1 + T_{kt}}{1 + \tau_{ikt}} .
$$

Values above one indicate more favourable treatment than the benchmark, and values below one indicate a relative disadvantage. Because $T_{kt}$ depends on the whole tariff structure,

$$
\Delta\tau_{ik} = 0 \;\not\Rightarrow\; \Delta btm_{ik} = 0 .
$$

Because $T_{kt}$ depends on $\sigma_k$, the measurement of market access cannot be separated from the behavioural parameters that govern trade adjustment.

### 4.2 Liberalization versus protection: erosion and amplification

Two stylised experiments involve exporters $A$ (preferential) and $B$. In each, $A$'s own tariff is unchanged, $\tau_{Ak}^1 = \tau_{Ak}^0$, and the other elements of the benchmark are held constant.

| Experiment | Shock | Benchmark | Effect on $A$ | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bilateral liberalization | $\tau_{Bk}$ falls | $T_k$ falls | $btm_{Ak}$ falls | **Preference erosion** |
| MFN protection | $\tau_{Bk}^{MFN}$ rises | $T_k$ rises | $btm_{Ak}$ rises | **Preference amplification** |

Tariff reforms therefore act through two channels:

- a **direct tariff effect**, from changes in an exporter's own tariff; and
- an **indirect competitive effect**, operating through the reference tariff and reaching suppliers whose statutory tariff is unchanged.

Under an MFN increase, a purely bilateral measure records *no* change for a preferential exporter, whereas the CES-consistent margin records an improvement; tariff increases can thus redirect trade towards untargeted exporters.

### 4.3 The four-step framework

The framework makes explicit every assumption between a tariff change and an interpretable trade effect. Its organising principle is that **measurement and estimation are not independent stages**: how the shock is constructed determines the variation to which the estimated response attaches.

$$
\text{Tariff shock} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Market access} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Elasticity} \;\rightarrow\; \text{Trade effect}
$$

**Step 1. Define the policy experiment.** The reform (a preferential concession, an MFN schedule change, a sectoral surcharge, or a broader reconfiguration) is defined by identifying both directly and indirectly affected suppliers, at the most disaggregated level at which treatment differs. Early aggregation obscures bilateral variation, and trade-weighted averages understate protection because high tariffs depress the imports that serve as weights.

**Step 2. Select the benchmark.** Alternative measures answer different questions and are not interchangeable:

- the bilateral tariff change measures the direct statutory modification;
- the MFN preference margin measures advantage relative to the MFN rate;
- a competitor-based average reflects rivals' treatment, but depends on the aggregation rule;
- the CES-consistent margin additionally accounts for substitution across origins.

The elasticity used to build the benchmark must be consistent with the elasticity used to translate margins into trade effects.

**Step 3. Estimate the trade elasticity.** The proposed specification, estimated by Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood (PPML), is

$$
E\left[ M_{ikt} \mid X \right] = \exp\left[ \beta_k \ln(btm_{ikt}) + \gamma_{it} + \delta_{kt} \right],
$$

with exporter-time effects $\gamma_{it}$ and product-time effects $\delta_{kt}$.

- PPML accommodates zero flows and, under standard conditions, remains consistent under heteroskedasticity.
- In this single-importer setting the specification identifies **conditional** responses and is not, by itself, a full general-equilibrium structural-gravity model.
- The coefficient's meaning depends on the regressor: with the bilateral tariff factor it measures the response to bilateral tariff costs; with the margin, the response to treatment *relative to the competitive benchmark*. Part of the third-country mechanism, an outcome in the diversion literature, thus moves into the measurement of the shock itself.
- Elasticities are heterogeneous across products. Since $T_{kt}$ depends on $\sigma_k$, the margin and the elasticity are interdependent. The authors propose an **iterative fixed-point procedure**:

$$
\text{initial benchmark} \rightarrow \widehat{\beta}^{(1)} \rightarrow \widehat{\sigma}^{(1)} \rightarrow T^{CES,(1)} \rightarrow btm^{(1)} \rightarrow \widehat{\beta}^{(2)} \rightarrow \widehat{\sigma}^{(2)} \rightarrow \cdots
$$

  The procedure starts, for example, from the MFN tariff and repeats until convergence, *subject to the existence and stability of a fixed point*. The mapping from $\beta_k$ to $\sigma_k$ depends on the structural model and must not be assumed independently of it.

**Step 4. Counterfactual evaluation.** Margins are computed under the baseline ($s=0$) and counterfactual ($s=1$) schedules:

$$
btm_{ik}^{s} = \frac{1 + T_k^{s}}{1 + \tau_{ik}^{s}}, \qquad s \in \lbrace 0, 1 \rbrace .
$$

A consistent counterfactual recomputes $T_k^1$ from the **complete** counterfactual schedule, not only from the tariffs that change directly. Following Lai and Zhu (2004), the proportional response and its value counterpart, applied to observed baseline trade $M_{ik}^0$, are

$$
\Delta Trade_{ik} = \frac{\widehat{M}_{ik}(btm_{ik}^1) - \widehat{M}_{ik}(btm_{ik}^0)}{\widehat{M}_{ik}(btm_{ik}^0)}, \qquad
TradeEffect_{ik} = M_{ik}^0 \cdot \Delta Trade_{ik} .
$$

The paper separates consistent *measurement* of the shock, which the CES margin ensures, from the model *closure*. A partial-equilibrium exercise holds expenditure and production fixed; a general-equilibrium exercise lets expenditure, output, factory-gate prices and multilateral resistance adjust. The two answer different policy questions and are not interchangeable.

## 5. Empirical Illustration: Data and Scenario

The illustration demonstrates the framework's mechanics; it is not a comprehensive evaluation of US trade policy.

- **Data.** Bilateral and **domestic** US trade flows come from the US International Trade and Production Database for Estimation (ITPDE); applied and MFN tariffs come from WITS–TRAINS. The data cover 2016–2021 and are harmonised across product and industry classifications (ISIC Rev. 3). Domestic US producers are included in the competitive benchmark even though domestic transactions bear no import tariff.
- **Scenario (February 2026).** The scenario is built from the Atlantic Council's Trump Tariff Tracker (accessed May 2026). It adds:
  - +10 percentage points for selected trading partners;
  - +15 points for Bolivia;
  - +25 points on steel and aluminium, and on motor vehicles and parts;
  - +50 points on copper.

  The sectoral measures apply across exporters. The scenario changes *applied* tariffs, not necessarily legally defined MFN rates.
- **Decomposition.** With $BTM_{ikt}^{s} = (1+T_{kt}^{s})/(1+\tau_{ikt}^{s})$ for $s \in \lbrace 0, CF \rbrace$, the change in the log margin splits exactly into two components:

$$
\Delta \ln BTM_{ikt} = \underbrace{-\Delta \ln(1+\tau_{ikt})}_{\text{own-tariff component}} + \underbrace{\Delta \ln(1+T_{kt})}_{\text{competitive-benchmark component}}
$$

- **Elasticities.** Substitution elasticities come from product-level PPML gravity estimates. Results are restricted to products with positive and statistically significant elasticities. Industries assigned the conventional elasticity of two are excluded.

## 6. Key Findings

### 6.1 Bilateral tariff margins by industry (Table 1)

Industry mean margins rise in **13 of the 17** reported industries and fall in **four**, where $\Delta BTM = (BTM_{SIM}/BTM_{CES} - 1) \times 100$. The reported industry-mean elasticities range from 2.78 (coke and refined petroleum) to 64.33 (medical, precision and optical instruments).

| Largest increases | $\Delta BTM$ (%) | Decreases | $\Delta BTM$ (%) |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Medical, precision and optical instruments | +7.88 | Wood and products of wood and cork | -5.91 |
| Food products and beverages | +4.07 | Fabricated metal products | -3.74 |
| Radio, television and communication equipment | +3.46 | Motor vehicles, trailers and semi-trailers | -3.03 |
| Rubber and plastics products | +3.11 | Crude petroleum and natural gas extraction | -1.11 |

A positive change does **not** mean that US protection fell. It means the CES benchmark rose relative to the tariffs faced by the suppliers represented. Each change combines own-tariff and competitor effects, weighted by market structure and substitution. Because the table compares ratios of industry averages, its percentages are neither average product-level changes nor an explicit numerical split of the two components of the decomposition.

- Among foreign suppliers, motor vehicles bear the largest losses, followed by chemicals and petroleum extraction. Textiles record sizeable foreign gains.
- **Domestic trade can move against foreign trade.** In medical instruments, modest net foreign gains coexist with a much larger domestic contraction; a similar offset appears in machinery and electrical equipment. Conversely, domestic gains in furniture and wood products offset foreign losses.
- An industry's net trade effect need not share the sign of its Table 1 margin change. Effects are computed at a finer level and then aggregated, so initial trade values, tariff changes and elasticities differ beneath the industry mean.
- The foreign/domestic split is an accounting decomposition by supplier origin, not the direct/indirect decomposition of the margin. It shows that an analysis restricted to imports would miss a substantial part of the adjustment.

## 7. Interpretive Boundaries

- The results illustrate transmission from tariffs to trade; they do not rank winners and losers from US protectionism.
- Effects are **partial-equilibrium** reallocations, conditional on the estimated responses and on other determinants of trade held fixed.
- They are **not** general-equilibrium effects, nor measures of welfare, output, employment or production.
- A full policy evaluation would also need to model adjustments in expenditure, prices, production and multilateral resistance.

## 8. Conclusion

Market access is relative: a tariff change affecting some suppliers reshapes the competitive environment of all others, including domestic producers. A bilateral tariff change therefore cannot by itself capture a reform's economic content, which depends on the benchmark against which the shock is measured.

Building on the preferential-margin and theory-consistent measurement literatures, the paper's four interdependent steps are: define the experiment; construct a CES-consistent measure of relative market access; estimate a mutually consistent elasticity; and translate the response into counterfactual trade effects.

Liberalization and protectionism fit one framework but are not mirror images: MFN cuts **erode** preferences, whereas MFN increases can **amplify** them by worsening the position of non-preferred suppliers. The US illustration shows heterogeneous margin changes, gains and losses coexisting within sectors and countries, and domestic adjustments that import-only analyses would miss. With overlapping preferences and renewed protectionism, making this relative dimension explicit is essential for economically meaningful estimates of trade-policy effects.
